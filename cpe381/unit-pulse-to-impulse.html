Definition of the unit pulse

For every positive width Δ we build a rectangular pulse centered at the origin:

pΔ(t) = { 1/Δ  for |t| ≤ Δ/2 ;  0 otherwise }

The rectangle is Δ wide and 1/Δ tall, so its area is Δ·(1/Δ) = 1 no matter how we choose Δ. This is why it is called a unit pulse. The two parameters trade off against each other: making the pulse narrower forces it to become taller by exactly the reciprocal factor, and that compensation is precisely what keeps the total area pinned at unity.

The limiting process

Now imagine squeezing the pulse: as Δ shrinks, the height 1/Δ grows. The pulse becomes a thinner and taller spike, yet the enclosed area never changes. In the limit the pulse is concentrated at a single point t = 0:

δ(t) = limΔ→0 pΔ(t)

The result cannot be an ordinary function: for t ≠ 0 it equals 0, while at t = 0 it would have to be infinite so that the area stays 1. It is therefore treated as a distribution, defined by what it does under an integral rather than by point values.

Properties inherited from the pulse

Every useful property of δ(t) is simply the limiting version of a property of pΔ(t):

• Unit area: ∫ pΔdt = 1  ⟹  ∫ δ(t)dt = 1
• Sifting: ∫ f(t)pΔ(t)dt ≈ f(0)  ⟹  ∫ f(t)δ(t)dt = f(0)
• Causality shift: pΔ(t−t₀)  ⟹  δ(t−t₀), a spike located at t₀

The sifting property

Multiply any smooth test signal f(t) by the pulse and integrate. Since pΔ vanishes outside a tiny window, only the behaviour of f near zero matters:

IΔ = ∫ f(t)·pΔ(t) dt = (1/Δ)∫|t|≤Δ/2 f(t) dt ≈ f(0)

The expression (1/Δ)∫f dt is exactly the average of f over the window. As Δ → 0 the average of a continuous function over a shrinking interval locks onto its central value f(0). In this demo f(t) = e−t²cos(2t), so the target is f(0) = 1, and the measured error decays proportionally to Δ².

Reading the plot

The grey curve is the test signal f(t). The violet shaded strip is the averaging window of current width Δ, and the cyan dashed segment marks the windowed average value IΔ. The amber dotted line is the destination f(0). As you animate Δ toward zero the strip tightens around the origin and the cyan line slides down onto the amber one — a literal picture of "the delta samples the signal at a single point".

Why engineers care

This construction is not a trick — it is how sampling, weighting and system analysis are made rigorous. Sampling a signal uses δ(t−nT); a system's impulse response h(t) is precisely what the LTI system outputs when fed this limiting spike; convolution with δ(t) returns the signal unchanged, because the delta carries unit area but zero duration. Every impulse in practice (a hammer tap, a voltage spike, a radar ping) is finite in time and power — i.e., one of these pulses — and the ideal δ(t) is the clean mathematical model obtained when the duration becomes negligible compared with the system's response time.
