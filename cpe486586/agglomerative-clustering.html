Agglomerative clustering, step by step

Agglomerative clustering starts with one cluster per point and merges the two closest clusters until a single cluster remains. The record of those merges is a tree, the dendrogram. A horizontal cut through the tree gives a flat clustering, and you can drag that cut once the run is over.

The algorithm and its math

Agglomerative clustering builds a hierarchy from the bottom up. It needs a distance between points and a rule, the linkage, for the distance between two clusters. Nothing else is required, and the number of clusters can be chosen after the run.

The algorithm

  1. Start. Put x1, …, xn in clusters of one point. Fill the n × n matrix with D(Ci, Cj) = ‖xi − xj‖. Ward's method stores the squared distance.
  2. Find. (A, B) = arg min D(P, Q) over pairs of distinct current clusters.
  3. Merge. Replace A and B with A ∪ B. Record a link of the dendrogram at height h = D(A, B), or h = √W(A, B) for Ward.
  4. Update. Delete the rows and columns of A and B. Add a row and a column for A ∪ B with the Lance–Williams formula.
  5. Repeat. Go back to the find step while more than one cluster remains. The run makes n − 1 merges.
  6. Cut. Choose a height h* and cut the dendrogram there. The subtrees hanging below the cut are the clusters.

The matrix holds n(n − 1)/2 distinct entries, so memory grows as O(n2). A plain search of the matrix costs O(n2) per merge, and n − 1 merges cost O(n3) in total. A priority queue lowers the time to O(n2 log n), and the SLINK algorithm computes single linkage in O(n2). This page uses the plain search, which is quick at ninety points.

Linkage, the distance between two clusters

single   D(A, B) = mina∈A, b∈B ‖a − b‖
complete   D(A, B) = maxa∈A, b∈B ‖a − b‖
average   D(A, B) = 1nA nB ∑a∈A ∑b∈B ‖a − b‖
Ward   W(A, B) = 2 nA nBnA + nB ‖μA − μB‖2

Here nA is the number of points in A and μA is their mean.

Single linkage needs one close pair, so a chain of points can tie two groups together. Complete linkage needs the farthest pair to be close, which keeps clusters compact and of similar diameter. Average linkage takes the mean over the nA nB pairs and lands between those two. Ward's method treats a merge as a cost, the rise in the within-cluster sum of squares, and so it prefers round groups of similar size.

For Ward, s = nA + nB + nK. The update reads old matrix entries and cluster sizes, so after the start the algorithm needs no coordinates at all.

With x = D(A, K) and y = D(B, K), single linkage gives ½(x + y) − ½|x − y| = min(x, y). The plus sign gives max(x, y), which is complete linkage. The panel beside the matrix runs the formula on the numbers of the current merge.

Ward's criterion

SSE(C) = ∑x∈C ‖x − μC‖2
ΔSSE = SSE(A ∪ B) − SSE(A) − SSE(B)
      = nA nBnA + nB ‖μA − μB‖2
W(A, B) = 2 ΔSSE,   h = √W(A, B)

The identity follows from μA∪B = (nAμA + nBμB)/(nA + nB) once the squares are expanded. Ward's method merges the pair with the smallest ΔSSE.

For two single points W is the squared distance, so the first link sits at the smallest Euclidean distance. The height √(2ΔSSE) is the convention of SciPy and scikit-learn.

Reading and cutting the dendrogram

h1 ≤ h2 ≤ … ≤ hn−1
hn−K ≤ h* < hn−K+1  ⇒  K clusters

The four linkages here are monotone. The reason is reducibility: when D(A, B) ≤ min(D(A, K), D(B, K)), the merged cluster satisfies D(A ∪ B, K) ≥ min(D(A, K), D(B, K)). Centroid and median linkage lack the property, and their dendrograms can show inversions, a link drawn lower than the links beneath it.

A large jump hn−K+1 − hn−K means the next merge would join groups that are far apart, so K is a natural choice there. The horizontal order of the leaves carries no meaning, since a link can swap its two branches without changing the tree.
